Why Does This Matter?

🎯 What does the attacker want?

To steal a password, payment details or personal information, deliver a malicious file, or pressure someone into taking an action.

⚡ Why does it work?

Because malicious emails and SMS messages can look credible, create urgency, and sometimes include real personal information to build trust.

🛡️ What stops it?

A short check before clicking: who sent it, what the domain is, whether the link is shortened, whether the request was expected, and whether it can be verified through a separate official channel.

Nine Main Signs of Phishing and Smishing

1. The sender looks familiar, but the domain does not

A similar-looking domain, an unnecessary addition, or an unrelated top-level domain appears instead of the real official site.

2. Stressful or urgent wording

“Your account will be blocked,” “You have an outstanding debt,” “Immediate approval required,” or “Click here now” are classic pressure techniques.

3. A link that does not lead to the official destination

Before clicking, check whether the domain actually belongs to the organization. For Israeli government services, you would normally expect gov.il or another official destination reached independently.

4. A shortened link hides the destination

TinyURL, Bitly, and similar services are not malicious by themselves, but they hide the final domain. In a payment, banking, or government message, that requires extra verification.

5. An unexpected attachment

Especially ZIP, HTML, EXE, DOCM, or a file from an unexpected source that demands unusual action.

6. An unusual request

A password reset, sharing an MFA code, urgent payment, entering card details, or signing in again are all warning signs.

7. Personal information does not prove the message is genuine

Your name, phone number, national ID number, or other details can come from a leaked dataset. Matching your personal details is not proof of legitimacy.

8. A local phone number or sender name is not proof

A phone number, Sender ID, or sending account can be spoofed, impersonated, or compromised.

9. Verify through an independent channel

If a message claims there is a debt, account block, or problem, do not use the message's link. Open the official website or app yourself and verify the claim there.

Email and SMS Examples to Review

Suspicious email example

From: Account Security <alerts@secure-account-check.com>

Subject: Verify your login immediately

Dear user, We noticed suspicious access. Please verify your login now to avoid suspension.

Example that requires verification

From: Delivery Update <notice@parcel-track.info>

Subject: Package delayed - open attachment

Your delivery has been delayed. Open the attached file for details.

Personalized smishing example

SMS: “Enforcement authority notice: an outstanding debt is under review. Reference: 35592. Payment: tinyurl.com/gov-pay [ID: ****1234]”

Why is this suspicious? The message creates urgency, uses a shortened link, and includes personal information to build trust. That information may come from a leak and does not prove the sender is legitimate.

Golden rule:

A bank, government service, card issuer, or delivery company asks you to act? Do not enter through the link you received. Open the official website or app yourself and check whether the same request actually exists.

What Should I Do If I Already Clicked?

1. Stop immediately

Do not keep clicking, open more files, or enter any additional information.

2. Change your password

If you entered a password on a suspicious page, change it immediately and also change it on other services where you used the same password.

3. Enable multi-factor authentication

If MFA is not enabled yet, enable it now. It is not perfect, but it makes the attacker's job significantly harder.

Rule of thumb:

If something feels too urgent, too frightening, or too good to be true, stop and verify it.
